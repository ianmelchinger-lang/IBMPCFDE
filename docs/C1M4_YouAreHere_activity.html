Would you start design against this list?

You are the enterprise AI deployment engineer for the Sample Enterprise, a fictional teaching case. Its insurance subsidiary has funded an assistant that drafts coverage determinations, which are the insurer’s decisions on claims, for North American claims. An adjuster approves every draft.

A half-day workshop of eight roles produced a list of 28 requirements, and every statement is signed. The claims operations director, who sponsors the work, wants the list adopted as the requirements register: the document that design, build and testing all work from. Cycle time, from receipt of a claim to its determination, stands at 9.4 calendar days against a promised 5.0. The AI review board must approve the assistant before it goes live. It sits every two weeks, and each missed sitting moves the date by two weeks.

Six of the 28 lines are below. For each one, decide whether you would start design against it today or hold it back. Commit your reading before anything is revealed.

    What the list leaves out

    The list says nothing about the EU region, either way. The claims operations director and the AI platform owner each assume a different answer. A reader cannot tell a region that was ruled out from one that was forgotten, so each reader fills the silence in their own way.

    The capability this list needs

    What the list needs is a capability, not a format. It is the ability to write requirements that a reviewer can test and an engineer can build against: each statement stripped of any solution chosen too early, traced to what imposes it, written so an observation decides it, and placed inside or outside the commitment.

    It has to be in place before design starts, because every later check is made against what the register says.
